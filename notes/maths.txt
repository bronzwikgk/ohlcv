The Volatility Ratio (Using Standard Deviation)
The Math: Short-Term SD (e.g., 21 days) / Long-Term SD (e.g., 100 days)

This ratio measures Volatility Expansion vs. Compression. It tells you if the stock's recent price swings are getting wider or tighter compared to its normal historical baseline.

Ratio < 0.6 (The "Coiled Spring" / Squeeze): The short-term volatility is unusually low compared to its history. The stock is tightly consolidating. This behavior signature usually precedes a massive, explosive breakout.
Ratio ≈ 1.0 (The "Steady Grinder"): The current volatility matches the historical baseline. The stock is behaving exactly as it normally does, making it highly predictable for trend-following.
Ratio > 1.5 (The "Exhaustion" / Erratic Climber): The short-term volatility is significantly higher than normal. The price action has become violent and erratic. This signature often indicates a climax top, panic selling, or a highly chaotic battle between buyers and sellers where stop-losses are easily hunted.
2. The Confidence Ratio (Using Standard Error)
The Math: Short-Term SE / Long-Term SE (Note: In trading, SE is best applied to the slope of a Linear Regression trendline, which measures the "noise" or "error" around the trend).

This ratio measures Trend Cleanliness and Conviction. It tells you if the recent trend is highly organized and mathematical, or if it is just a messy byproduct of random noise.

Ratio < 0.5 (The "Institutional Channel"): The short-term error is much lower than the long-term error. The recent price action is extremely clean, hugging the trendline perfectly with very little noise. This implies strong, calculated institutional accumulation or distribution.
Ratio > 1.5 (The "Retail Chop"): The short-term error is much higher than the long-term error. Even if the stock is technically going up, it is bouncing wildly around the trendline. The trend lacks conviction and is driven by emotion or thin liquidity.
